Spacecraft Cabin Fire

A 3D fire model inside a crewed spacecraft. It has three pressurised elements: a US-Lab-style module with a ring of equipment racks around a central aisle, a node with two crew quarters and a galley, and an airlock. Pick where a fire starts. Each location is a real kind of spacecraft item with its own material, and you can swap the material to see how the fire's behaviour changes. The engine solves Navier–Stokes airflow, mixing-controlled combustion, P1 radiation, heat conduction into thick panels, and the toxic gases CO, HCN and HF. It models CO₂ and water-mist extinguishers, venting to vacuum, and four crew members who move and breathe the smoke. It is checked against the Saffire flight fires.

Fire origin
Open flame lights the item directly. Overheat heats it from inside at 15 kW/m² (smoulders and smokes) or 40 kW/m² (can ignite), as an overloaded wire would. Cut power stops the heating.
Crew response
Extinguishers discharge at the selected fire origin: about 2.7 kg of CO₂ over 45 s, or 2 kg of fine water mist over 50 s. Masks are oxygen-supplied breathing sets that last 15 minutes.
Last resort. Close the hatches first, or the whole vehicle vents. NASA tests found venting slow and able to fan a fire before it goes out (NTRS 20260003546).
Cabin atmosphere
ISS cabin requirement: 8–20 cm/s of air movement in the aisle.

Crew

fractional effective dose, ISO 13571 / Purser

Each crew member breathes the gas at their position. Dose adds up from CO, HCN, low O₂ partial pressure and heat, with CO₂ speeding up breathing. A dose of 0.3 marks the start of impairment and 1 marks incapacitation. After an alarm the crew move to a safe module: about 15 s to react, or 60 s if asleep. A closed hatch blocks them.

    The model

    The same equations as the habitat model, solved on a 3D grid that holds the spacecraft's cylindrical pressure shells (4.2 m and 2.6 m diameter), its racks, bulkheads and crew-quarters enclosures. Ventilation enters through overhead diffusers, a rack cooling fan and crew-quarters fans, and leaves through deck return grilles, so a fire's smoke follows the real cabin airflow.

    1. Momentum, buoyancy and turbulence.∂u/∂t + (u·∇)u = −∇p̃ + g (T/T₀ − 1) ĵ + ∇·[(ν + νt)∇u], νt = (0.18 Δ)² |S|
    2. Projection with thermal expansion.∇·u = q̇‴net/(ρ cp T), sealed compartments: dP/dt = (γ − 1) Q̇/V

      Closing a hatch or a crew-quarters door traps smoke and pressure in that compartment.

    3. Combustion and smoke.q̇‴ = ρ ΔHO₂ min(YF, YO₂)/τmix, ΔHO₂ = 13.1 MJ/kg, TCFT = 1600 K, visibility = 3/K

      Each material has its own heat of combustion (as O₂ demand) and smoke yield. Wire insulation and foams are much sootier than cotton.

    4. Flame existence at a surface.Tf = Tg + κ XO₂[1 − R(101.3/P)/(U + Ud)], flame if Tf ≥ Tc and U ≤ Ubo

      U is the ventilation air speed at the item plus the flame's own buoyant flow. Each material's Tc is set by its limiting oxygen index.

    5. Spread.Vopp = (π/4) λ(Tf − Tv)/[m″cs(Tv − Tg)], Vcon ∝ Us (Saffire-1 calibrated, scaled by m″cs(Tv − T₀))
    6. Radiation (P1).−∇·(∇G / 3κ) + κG = 4κσT⁴ (or χrq̇‴ in flames), κ = 0.03 + 1862 fvT + κgas, walls: −Γ∂G/∂n = ε/(2(2−ε)) (G − 4σTw⁴)

      Radiation from flames and soot heats nearby surfaces and is absorbed by walls, so far-off items can preheat.

    7. Thick panels.ρc ∂T/∂t = k ∂²T/∂x² (8 nodes, implicit), burning: ṁ″ = (q̇″flame + q̇″rad − k ∂T/∂x|s)/Lg

      PMMA, polycarbonate and silicone heat up through their depth. Concurrent spread on them follows V = kt U q̇″²/(kρc ΔT²), calibrated to Saffire VI-3.

    8. Toxic products.YCO = y0g + (y1g − y0g)√(g/g₀), rising toward 0.2 when the compartment runs short of O₂; smoke ∝ P

      Flames in 0 g make far more CO than on Earth. Saffire cotton fabrics gave about 0.034 kg/kg, against 0.004 in 1 g. Overheated PTFE gives off HF.

    9. Suppression and venting.CO₂: dilutes O₂ and raises heat capacity (Xeff = X/(1 + 0.6xCO₂)); mist: evaporates on available heat, wets surfaces; vent: dP/dt = −P CdA c*/V
    10. Crew dose.FED = Σ[2.76·10⁻⁵ CO¹·⁰³⁶ + (eHCN/43/220 − 0.0045)] vCO₂ Δt + Σ Δt/e8.13−0.54(20.9−O₂) + Σ Δt/(5·10⁷ T−3.4)

      Times in minutes and concentrations at sea-level-equivalent partial pressure, so a depressurised module is lethal through hypoxia.

    11. Electrical overheat.m″cs dTs/dt = q″overheat + h(Tg − Ts) + εgσ(Tg⁴ − Ts⁴) − εσ(Ts⁴ − T₀⁴)

      Above the pyrolysis temperature the insulation gives off fuel vapour and dense smoke. At 40 kW/m² the hot surface acts as a pilot, so a flame appears if the material can burn in that atmosphere. At 15 kW/m² it only smoulders, which is the smoke-first event spacecraft detectors are designed to catch.

    Methods and data sources

      What this model still cannot do

      • The cabin layout is representative, not a specific vehicle. Real racks hold hundreds of items and the airflow inside racks is far more complex.
      • Except for SIBAL and PMMA, material limits come from typical handbook LOI values, not from flight data. Treat those materials as indicative.
      • Cells of 12–15 cm cannot resolve a flame. Surface flames are subgrid models, and a burning item is at least one cell in size. The grid-convergence check shows how far results move with cell size.
      • Toxic gases come from fixed yields, not chemistry. Only cellulose fabrics have measured 0 g CO and smoke yields (Saffire). Other materials use 6× their 1 g CO yield and their 1 g smoke yield.
      • Extinguisher and mist amounts are representative, not hardware specifications. The discharge is a volume source near the item, not a resolved jet.
      • Against Saffire, the model underpredicts the IV-1 spread rate by about 40% and overpredicts cotton spread by about 70%. It sounds the smoke alarm 2–4× too early at reduced pressure.
      • Crew behaviour is a simple rule: react, then move to the other module. Real crews follow procedures and fight the fire.
